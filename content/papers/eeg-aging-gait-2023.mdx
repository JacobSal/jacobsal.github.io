## Why this paper matters

Walking is far more cognitively demanding than we typically appreciate. Every step requires split-second decisions — weight shifting, obstacle avoidance, environmental awareness — all while performing other cognitive tasks. As we age, this dance between brain and body becomes increasingly precarious, and falls become one of the leading causes of injury and death in older adults.

This study grew out of a simple observation in the lab: older adults *look* like they're thinking harder when they walk. Their attention narrows, their conversation slows, and their gait becomes more variable. We wanted to quantify what the brain is actually doing differently.

## Approach

We used **mobile EEG** — wireless electrode caps that let participants walk freely — combined with motion capture to record 30 younger (18–30 yrs) and 30 older (65–80 yrs) adults during:

1. Single-task walking (just walk)
2. Dual-task walking (walk while performing a verbal fluency task)

The key innovation here is that we recorded the full EEG time series **while participants were actually moving**, not just before or after. This gives us a window into real-time brain dynamics during locomotion — something traditional lab-based studies miss.

## Key findings

- **Theta power** (4–8 Hz) in frontal regions was significantly reduced in older adults during dual-task conditions. Theta is thought to reflect working memory and executive control demands.
- **Alpha suppression** — typically a marker of active cognitive processing — was attenuated in older adults, suggesting less dynamic neural recruitment.
- Gait variability (step-time variability) was strongly correlated with frontal theta deficits, supporting the idea that gait control draws on prefrontal executive resources.

## What this means

These findings add to a growing body of evidence that fall risk in older adults has a neural fingerprint — one that could potentially be detected before falls occur. EEG biomarkers during walking may eventually serve as sensitive, non-invasive tools for clinical screening, helping clinicians identify at-risk individuals earlier.

## What comes next

We are now running a longitudinal follow-up (see the *Mind in Motion* project) to test whether baseline EEG markers predict incident falls over a 12-month period. If so, we may have a powerful new tool for fall prevention.
