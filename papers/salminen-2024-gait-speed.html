Gait Speed Related Changes in Electrocortical Activity in Younger and Older Adults

Salminen J, Liu C, Pliner EM, Tenerowicz M, Roy A, Richer N, Hwang J, Hass CJ, Clark DJ, Seidler RD, Manini TM, Cruz-Almeida Y, Ferris DP

Venue · Journal of Neurophysiology Vol. · 133(6), 1761–1794 (2024) DOI · 10.1152/jn.00544.2024
Read on publisher ↗

The plain-language version

Walking faster takes more than stronger muscles — it takes more from the brain. As gait speed increases, the cortex changes how it organizes activity to keep balance and rhythm. This paper traces those changes and asks whether they look the same in older adults as in younger ones.

We had younger and older adults walk on a treadmill across a parametric range of gait speeds while we recorded high-density EEG and full-body kinematics. The analysis follows source-localized cortical activity as speed increases, and compares the speed-dependent slopes between age groups.

Why this matters

Gait speed is one of the strongest single predictors of mobility decline and mortality in aging. Most of the science explaining why focuses on the legs and the cardiovascular system. This paper adds the missing piece: how the cortex itself reorganizes with speed, and how that reorganization differs with age. That gives us a candidate neural signature for clinical assessment of mobility risk.

What we found

Cortical activity in motor and parietal regions scales systematically with gait speed, and the scaling differs in older adults — pointing toward an age-related change in how the cortex contributes to the control of walking. Full results, including the bands and source clusters that drove the effect, are in the manuscript.
